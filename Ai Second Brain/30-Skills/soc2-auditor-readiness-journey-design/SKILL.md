# SOC 2 Auditor Readiness Journey Design

## Outcome

Take a small team from "interested in SOC 2" to a report a buyer can trust, without wasting months on the wrong firm, the wrong scope, or a tool bought before the controls exist: a staged journey with a decision at each stage, a cost discipline that refuses to budget from estimates, and a trust pack that is honest at every stage.

## Design

1. **Readiness assessment first.** An independent — or brutally honest self — assessment against the elected criteria: scope, system description draft, control matrix, and evidence gaps rated by severity and effort. This is advisory only, not an attestation. Industry guidance in the 2026-11-02 research run puts readiness 3–6 months before a Type II observation period begins; treat that as guidance, not a rule, and let the auditor set the period.
2. **Select an auditor from licensed CPA firms only.** Evaluate: SOC 2 SaaS experience, peer-review status, independence, report-sample quality (do they list exceptions clearly?), buyer recognition (will target customers accept a boutique firm, or do they require a known name?), and fee plus scope in writing. Interview two or three firms; ask for a redacted sample report and a fixed-fee proposal by criteria scope. A firm that also sells the compliance platform or readiness work for the same engagement is an independence red flag. A non-CPA "SOC 2 certifier" yields no SOC 2 report, however polished its badge looks.
3. **Run a practice period, then start the clock.** Build or close the controls, write truthful policies, and wire evidence collection so it runs continuously. Trace a mock sample before the observation period. Controls implemented during the period only evidence the post-implementation part.
4. **Understand the timelines honestly.** Type I, in broader guidance, runs from 1–2 months of readiness plus 2–4 weeks of fieldwork for a well-prepared cloud-native team, to 4–6 months end to end. Type II runs from 1–3 months of readiness, plus an observation period of 3–12 months (6 most common first time, 12 for renewals and larger organisations), plus 4–10 weeks of fieldwork and reporting — 6–18 months from kickoff in total, depending on scope, readiness, and auditor scheduling. Ranges disagree across sources because scope, readiness, and scheduling vary; treat any single timeline as an estimate, and let the auditor set the observation period. A vendor promising to "compress" the observation window is misrepresenting the standard.
5. **Deliver the report correctly.** The auditor issues the report; share it under NDA with prospects and customers, answer questionnaires from it, and publish only a SOC 3 or trust-centre summary publicly. Between reports, issue management bridge letters (gap letters) covering the period from report end to the request date — management-signed, stating no material changes or no known breaches, typically carrying about 3 months of credibility in the research run's account, and not an auditor opinion. Maintain controls continuously; the next Type II period starts immediately, usually 12 months.
6. **Maintain annually, or the next audit costs more.** The annual cycle is: reviews, risk reassessment, vendor re-reviews, penetration testing where buyers require it, restore tests, and policy re-attestation. Letting controls lapse between audits makes the next Type II more expensive than maintaining them.
7. **Build the trust pack before the report exists.** A customer trust pack unblocks diligence before a full audit: complementary controls customers must implement, data-processing agreement, subprocessor list, security page, questionnaire-response library, and a SOC 3-style summary — without claiming a report that does not exist. Never claim "SOC 2 certified" or vendor inheritance in the trust pack. Buyer leverage without audit cost is the point; misrepresentation destroys it.

## Cost discipline

- **No AICPA price list exists, and no credible one-person SOC 2 price was found in the 2026-11-02 research run.** Do not budget from third-party ranges, and do not extrapolate per-head from 50–250-person totals: auditor minimums, fixed platform fees, and fixed documentation work mean a solo audit will not be a fraction of a larger company's audit.
- Third-party ranges for small-to-mid SaaS in that research run, for planning only: first-year all-in around $20k–$80k in one analysis, with auditor fees alone $10k–$35k for boutique firms versus $60k–$150k+ for Big 4 firms, and compliance platforms starting around $7k–$12k per year before audit fees. Those figures are for 20–150-person companies, are vendor and blog estimates, and conflict across sources.
- **Replace every range with two or three fixed auditor quotes** (Security-only versus Security plus Availability) before any budget decision. Match firm tier to buyer requirements, not prestige.
- **Do not buy a compliance platform first.** Buy a platform only when a Type II observation period is committed, evidence volume exceeds manual cadence, or an auditor or buyer explicitly expects continuous monitoring evidence. Record the deferral as a dated decision note ("platform deferred until [trigger], because [reason]") — that note is itself good evidence that tooling decisions were reasoned, not defaulted. See the catalog shortlist for platform shapes and limits.

## Guardrails

- Do not engage the readiness firm as the auditor. Independence is not a nicety; it is a condition of a credible report.
- Do not let a platform's green dashboard hide policy-versus-practice gaps. A platform automates evidence collection; it does not create controls, fix infrastructure, run reviews, or issue a report.
- Do not publish the SOC 2 report publicly. It is restricted-use, shared under NDA; publish only the SOC 3 summary or trust-centre summary.
- Do not let a stale report sit. A report whose period ended many months ago, with no bridge letter, loses buyer value fast.
- Do not promise a timeline or a price the auditor has not confirmed. Scope, readiness, and auditor scheduling move both.

## Verification

A journey plan is ready when: readiness gaps are rated with owners and dates; two or three licensed CPA firms have been interviewed with fixed-fee proposals by scope; the Type I versus Type II choice and observation period are auditor-confirmed (or explicitly marked unconfirmed); a practice period has been traced without gaps; the trust pack answers questionnaires from real artefacts without over-claiming; and no budget line rests on a third-party range rather than a quote.
